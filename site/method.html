Method and research

Beatn’ the Books compares a rating model with the betting market, then tests every idea on seasons it never saw. The short version: the NFL closing line is very hard to beat. One signal, wind, has held up so far.

1. The market price

A moneyline becomes a probability with q = 1 / d, where d is decimal odds. Both sides add up to more than 100%; the extra is the book's margin. Removing it gives the fair price:

pfair = qhome / (qhome + qaway)

Expected value always uses the price you'd actually get: Expected return (EV) = pmodel · d − 1, where d is the decimal odds. Break-even at −110 is 52.4%.

2. The Elo model

Every team has a rating, updated after each game since 1999 with a margin-of-victory multiplier. Each season pulls ratings halfway back to 1500. Settings were tuned on 2006–2015 only: K = 20, home field 55 Elo, rest 6 Elo per day (capped at ±35), and a 75-Elo penalty when a team starts someone other than its usual QB.

Phome = 1 / (1 + 10−Δ/400), Δ = Rhome + HFA + adj − Raway

The "Elo lean" blends the model with the market, 22% Elo and 78% market, and flags a side when that blend is worth betting (a positive expected return) at the posted price.

4. Searching for an edge against the market

Instead of building a better standalone model, each idea was tested on top of the market's own price. If a signal adds nothing the market hasn't priced, its coefficient is zero.

logit P(outcome) = b0 + b1 · logit(pmarket) + b2 · signal
  • The rule was fixed in advance. A signal had to pass on 2001–2015 (|z| ≥ 2, same sign in both halves). Only then was 2016–2025 scored, once.
  • 37 signals across moneylines, spreads and totals: rest, travel and time zones, divisional and home underdogs, last week's result, season against the spread, QB changes, referees, temperature, wind, and more.
  • 8 injury signals were added later (2013–2025): snap share of players listed Out, Doubtful or Questionable, and starting QBs out. The market clearly moves on injury reports, but none passed the screen: by the close, injuries are fully priced.
  • Moneyline and spread: nothing. Elo disagreement and the QB flag passed the screen, then lost to the closing line out of sample.
  • Totals: wind. The totals model was the only one to beat the closing line out of sample, and wind did the work.

5. The wind signal

Bet the under when an outdoor game's forecast wind, averaged over the kickoff hour and the three hours after, is 10 mph or more. Retractable roofs don't count.

6. Betting earlier in the week

A second historical dataset (opening and closing prices, 2014–2025) shows the market learns during the week, and the two signals here point the same way it moves:

  • Closing lines are sharper than openers, slightly at Pinnacle and clearly at the softer books (bet365, betr).
  • Lines moved toward the model’s leans 58% of the time against bet365's openers (671 moved lines) and 73% against betr's (88). The same leans that lost 6.5% at bet365's close were about break-even at its open.
  • Windy-game totals drop more during the week (about −1.0 point vs −0.4 for calm games at bet365), and unders paid more at the open than at the close in every period. This uses recorded wind, which nobody has days ahead, so it overstates what a bettor could get.
  • The site now records the first and last Kalshi/Polymarket price for every game, to measure this on the markets you can actually use.

7. Data and updates

  • Games, scores, lines and projected starters: nflverse games.csv. Lines usually appear a day or two before kickoff.
  • Wind: Open-Meteo forecasts for each stadium, refreshed on every update and frozen at kickoff.
  • Best prices (when set up): The Odds API, every major US sportsbook. For upcoming games the market number is the median no-vig price across books, and leans are priced at the best book. Finished games are always scored at the nflverse closing line, the same way the backtests were.
  • The site rebuilds from fresh data several times a day. Every number on it is computed by the same Python code that ran the backtests.
